How it works

  1. Terrain. For every 20 m spot in a 2 × 2 km site, MARGEN traces the horizon in 360 directions across NASA LRO LOLA terrain: 20 m detail nearby, 80 m out to 250 km, with the Moon's curvature.
  2. Sky. JPL DE440 ephemerides (NAIF SPICE) give the Sun and Earth every hour from January to mid-April 2027. Sunlight counts the visible part of the Sun's disc; Earth in view needs Earth's center above the terrain. Solar panels and antenna sit 2 m above the ground.
  3. Landings. 1,000 landing points scatter around the aim with a 100 m typical miss: half fall closer than that. A landing works if the ground slopes 10° or less across 40 m; in its first 24 hours the Sun is up at least 50% of the time and Earth is in view at least 30%; and over its first 14 days it meets the same two shares. Landing is at 00:00 UTC. With 1,000 landings the chance is accurate to ±3.1 points or better (95%).

What it doesn't model

  • Rocks, small craters and slopes smaller than the 20 m map spots. Athena's crater is one of them. Slope is measured across 40 m, so ground that passes can still be too steep at the scale of a lander.
  • Lander tilt, dust, temperature and batteries.
  • Radio links. Earth in view is a clear line to Earth; it does not check antennas or ground stations.
  • Light during the descent, and landing times other than 00:00 UTC.
  • How long the lander survives in the dark. The sunlight needs are shares of time, not unbroken hours.
  • Real landing errors are not the same in every direction, and some recent landers have missed by more. MARGEN uses a 100 m typical miss in every direction.
  • Terrain more than 250 km away.
